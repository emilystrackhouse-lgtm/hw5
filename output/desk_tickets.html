Reflection

Based on the Problem 9 run, recorded in the ticket tabs, the Cash tab, output/resolved_tickets.json and output/audit_trail.json. I also refer to the two earlier test runs from Problem 5, which are written up in output/harness.md.

1. How the agents performed on each ticket

Ticket 101 (Tauhid's size S tee): right answer, inefficient path. The most important call was getting this ticket right, and the team got it right. Inventory read invoice 501's description, "Rush reprint CC-TEE-WHITE S," and concluded the tee had already been ordered: the fix was paying the $840 bill, not buying another tee. Accounting filed exactly one request (#1, $840). After I approved it, checking went from $3,400 to $2,560, invoice 501 was marked paid, and the ticket resolved. That matters because the agents didn't make this connection on their own at first: in the first Problem 5 test run they requested both the $840 invoice and an $8 duplicate tee order. They only caught it after I added the "check open invoice descriptions before ordering" rule to the Inventory, Accounting and Boss prompts. So the good result came from a lesson I had to teach, not from instinct.

The process was the least efficient of the three. The Boss asked Accounting twice: first "review invoice 501, don't create a request," then "now create the request." The invoice was checked four separate times with check_invoice_and_vendor_hold (once by Inventory, twice by Accounting, once by Customer Service), and the run cost 67,813 tokens, the most of the three. The customer draft was polite and promised no date, but it told Tauhid we're "seeking approval to release the already-described Rush reprint." That's more of our internal process than a customer needs, even though it never mentions money owed. My grade: strong outcome, average execution.

Ticket 102 (rent): the cleanest run. This one went almost exactly to plan. The Boss handed it to Facilities. Facilities checked the email's "due in 2 days" against lease 1 ($2,400, due 2026-09-02) and then handed the payment to Accounting itself. That's the nested Facilities → Accounting hand-off I had predicted. Accounting filed one rent request (#2), Facilities wrote the landlord draft, and nobody pulled in Inventory or Customer Service. After I approved it, checking dropped from $2,560 to $160 and the lease's next due date moved to 2026-10-02. Accounting even reported the right "balance after" ($160), because it saw the live $2,560 balance left after ticket 101. The weakness was cost relative to difficulty: 44,133 tokens, with the Boss alone using 59% of them, and the rent checked three times (Boss, Facilities, Accounting) for a task that is basically one lookup and one request. My grade: excellent outcome, clean delegation, heavy overhead.

Ticket 103 (Yale AI Club hoodies): the best judgment. This was the hardest ticket because it is the cash squeeze from my Problem 2 analysis made real. Inventory found 8 of 20 size M in stock. It also correctly reported that Bulldog Print Co was no longer on hold, because ticket 101's payment had cleared invoice 501. Accounting priced three discounts (10%, 15%, 20%), recommended 15% ($49.30 each, 55.4% margin), and flagged that the $264 restock would overdraw the $160 left by $104. Nobody filed a purchase order that would have been refused. The Boss approved the 15% offer for the 8 in stock and resolved the ticket. Customer Service wrote one draft that said "your request for a bulk discount" with no invented percentage. That too was a fix from the test runs: earlier drafts put "10% off" in the club's mouth, and in the second test run the Boss wrote a competing draft of its own. The run used 55,772 tokens, and the Boss again used over half (53%). Stock was looked up three times and discounts priced four times, because Customer Service re-checked the 15% figure itself. My grade: best business judgment of the three. The cash conflict was handled by not spending, which is exactly right when no money comes in.

3. What would have been simpler with one agent and the same tools

  • Ticket 102A single agent could have done this in about five MCP calls: get_ticket → check_rent_due → request_payment_approval → post_to_board → update_ticket_status. The team used 13 MCP calls plus 2 hand-offs, checked the rent three times, and spent 44,133 tokens, 59% of them on the Boss coordinating. The rent ticket has no judgment that needs a specialist.
  • Gathering factsEvery hand-off loses the underlying data, so the next agent looks it up again: invoice 501 four times in 101, hoodie stock three times and the 15% discount twice in 103. One agent would have looked each fact up once and kept it in context.
  • Customer draftsCustomer Service had to rebuild facts the Boss already had, and in the second test run the Boss wrote its own draft anyway. One agent writing the reply right after deciding would have avoided both the re-checking and the conflicting-drafts problem I had to fix in code.
  • Coordination costThe Boss used 45–59% of every run's tokens mostly to route work and restate answers. With one agent, that overhead disappears.
  • Where the team earned its keepSpecialization helped on the judgment calls. Inventory's focused prompt is what caught the duplicate tee order, and Accounting's spending ranking is what kept the $264 restock from overdrawing the account. Splitting roles also made permissions clear: only Accounting can file payment requests, and only Customer Service can write customer drafts. I should be honest, though: the money safety comes from the MCP server (the human-only record_human_decision, amounts taken from the database, no negative balances), not from having five agents. One agent with the same tools would have been just as safe with cash.

What I'd take away

The final run did what the shop needed. All three tickets resolved, $3,240 went out only through two human Approve clicks, the balance reconciled to $160 in cash_accounts, and the one purchase that would have overdrawn us was never even requested. The agents got there partly because of guardrails I added after watching them fail: the duplicate-order rule, the "don't misquote the requester" rule, and code that stops one agent from writing another's messages. My biggest lesson is that the expensive part of a multi-agent team is not the thinking but the hand-offs. Every delegation re-checked the same facts, and the Boss spent about half of every run coordinating. For simple tickets like rent, I would route straight to one capable agent and save the full team for tickets like 103, where several judgments (stock, margin, cash priority, customer wording) actually pull against each other.
